Love in the Context of Climate Change — An Indigenous Relationship to the Earth

Love in the context of climate change can best be understood from an Indigenous perspective, through the shared values that they live by. While Indigenous communities have various cultural and historical differences, they are united in their reciprocal and symbiotic relationship with Mother Earth. In this relationship, there is no separation between humans and the Earth, so their idea of family is extended to flora, fauna, and all other living beings. It is with this sentiment that community is embodied and their love is extended beyond their own people.

Kincentric ecology, or kincentricity, delineates this relationship of absolute interconnectedness with all life forms, not just the human. However, the English language and Indigenous languages do not have the same purposes, and thus have different linguistic tools available. In fact, kincentric ecology is only our understanding of such a relationship but holds little significance for someone of Indigenous heritage and experience. Kincentric ecology is like the Rarámuri concept of iwígara, it is like the Māori concept of kaitiakitanga, like the Diné concept of hózhó; it exists in some form for all Indigenous people as it is the very core of their way of life. Hózhó (ho-ZHO), for example, is roughly translated from Diné Bizaad to “joy of being a part of the beauty of all creation”. It reflects a broader ethos of a people centered in connectedness, harmony, and balance with the natural Earth. In a world increasingly individualistic, where the separation between humans and Earth has been purposeful, most languages like English do not carry the words to describe such a relationship. To live in harmony with the Earth can only be learned through experience, traditions, and stories. It is much more than a concept to be debated; kincentricity is an active form of loving.
